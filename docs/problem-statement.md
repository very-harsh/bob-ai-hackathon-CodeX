# Problem Statement

## Who is affected
- **Investigating officers and scene-of-crime teams**, who collect large numbers of items under time pressure and must decide what is sent to the lab first.
- **Forensic science laboratory (FSL) intake staff**, who receive large unranked submissions.
- **Victims and families**, whose cases slow down when critical evidence waits in the queue.

## The problem
At a serious crime scene, the volume of material is far bigger than what can be tested at once. The hackathon problem statement cites the 2019 Hyderabad veterinarian murder case, where investigators received 3,000+ photos and 200+ physical items. Without prioritisation, a DNA-bearing cigarette butt was nearly missed in the first sweep, and overloaded FSLs delay testing by weeks.

The decision about what to test first is made manually, and it depends on the experience of whoever is at the scene.

## Why existing approaches fall short
- Manual triage is inconsistent between officers and between cases.
- Mundane-looking items (butts, bottles, fabric scraps) are easy to under-rank, yet can carry the most identifying evidence.
- Time-sensitive evidence (biological material, weather-exposed marks, phones that can be wiped remotely, CCTV that overwrites itself) is not always pulled ahead of durable evidence.
- Even a fixed priority table cannot see the scene: the same item can matter more or less depending on where it was found and what conditions it is in.
- FSLs receive lists with no reasoning attached, so they cannot quickly see why an item is urgent.

## Why it matters now
Digital and biological evidence are both time-critical, and case volumes keep growing. A consistent, explainable triage step at the start of the chain gives the lab a clear order of work from day one.

## Figures used
The figures above (3,000+ photos, 200+ items, testing delays of weeks) come from the hackathon problem statement. We have not independently verified them.
